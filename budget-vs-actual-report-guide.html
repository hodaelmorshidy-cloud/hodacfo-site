The Budget vs Actual Report a Board Actually Trusts

The variance bridge, an accrual-to-cash reconciliation, and a rolling reforecast that stays in sync with the actuals feeding it — explained by a Financial Controller.

By Hoda Elmorshidy • Fractional CFO • Financial Modeling • Corporate Finance since 2017

Most budget vs actual reports answer "how much did we miss by." The version a board actually trusts answers "why," in a way that ties out to the number above it and does not require a follow-up meeting to explain. That gap — between a variance table and a variance bridge — is a structural choice made in the workbook, not a formatting choice made in the deck.

Start From a Driver, Not a Line Item

A budget built as a single revenue line with a growth-rate assumption cannot explain a miss when it happens — there is nothing underneath the number to point to. A driver-based budget builds revenue as units × price by product or segment, so when actual revenue comes in above or below plan, the model already knows how much of the gap is more units sold, how much is a different price realized, and how much is a mix shift between higher- and lower-margin products.

Budgeted Revenue = ∑ (Budget Units × Budget Price) across segments
Actual Revenue    = ∑ (Actual Units × Actual Price) across segments

The three-way decomposition of that gap into price, volume and mix effects is genuinely non-trivial to get right — the interaction term is easy to misattribute. That mechanic is covered in full, with a worked numeric example, in our Variance Analysis & PVM Bridge guide. This article focuses on what happens once that decomposition exists: how it feeds a report a board can actually use.

The Bridge, Not the Table

A variance table lists budget, actual, and the delta for every line. A variance bridge walks from budget to actual visually, one driver at a time, so the reader sees which few drivers explain almost all of the gap without scanning forty rows. In practice, three to five drivers usually explain 80–90% of any given month's total variance — the bridge's job is to surface exactly those and let everything else sit quietly in an "other" bucket.

The rule that keeps a bridge honest

Every driver bar in the bridge must sum exactly to the total variance at the far right. If it doesn't — if there's a plug, a rounding gap, or an "other" bucket doing more work than it should — the bridge is decorative, not diagnostic, and the first sophisticated question in the board meeting will find the gap.

Budget vs Latest Estimate: The Question That Actually Matters Mid-Year

By month six, "did we beat the original January budget" is a much less useful question than "are we on track against what we now believe the year will be." A budget-to-latest-estimate bridge answers the second question directly: it isolates how much of the current-month variance is a genuine in-month result versus a change in the full-year forecast made last month. Reporting only the original-budget comparison after the halfway point of the year tends to either overstate a win (the original budget was conservative) or manufacture a crisis (the original budget was aggressive) that the reforecast already knows about.

The Accrual-to-Cash Bridge: Where Most Reports Stop Short

A P&L-level variance report explains earnings. It does not explain cash, and a board asking "why is the bank balance not matching the P&L story" deserves an answer built from the same model, not a separate ad hoc calculation.

Cash from Operations ≈ Net Income
   − Increase in Accounts Receivable (driven by DSO)
   + Increase in Accounts Payable (driven by DPO)
   ± other working-capital and non-cash items

A rising DSO (days sales outstanding) means revenue is being recognized faster than it is being collected — earnings look fine while cash lags behind. A rising DPO (days payable outstanding) does the opposite, flattering near-term cash at the cost of stretching suppliers. A budget vs actual report that bridges earnings to cash through DSO and DPO turns "why doesn't the bank balance match the P&L" from a recurring surprise into a line item that was already forecast.

A Worked Result

In the Annual Budget & Reforecast Model, one shipped month shows revenue beating plan by $27,126 — and the decomposition shows only $3,036 of that was genuinely a price effect.
