Why Ranking Projects by NPV Alone Misallocates a Budget

NPV, IRR, profitability index, risk-adjusted hurdle rates, and how capital rationing actually decides which projects get funded — explained by a Financial Controller.

By Hoda Elmorshidy • Fractional CFO • Financial Modeling • Corporate Finance since 2017

Capital budgeting answers "should we do this project." Capital allocation answers a harder question: given a fixed budget and more good projects than money to fund them, which ones actually get built. Most free NPV templates only answer the first question — they say yes or no to one project in isolation, with no concept of a budget constraint or a queue of competing submissions.

NPV: The Foundation

NPV = ∑ CFt / (1 + r)t − Initial Outlay

Net Present Value discounts every future cash flow back to today at the project's cost of capital and nets out the initial investment. A positive NPV means the project creates value above what the capital could earn elsewhere at the same risk. This is the textbook decision rule, and on a single, unconstrained project it is the right one.

Why NPV Alone Breaks Down Under a Budget Constraint

NPV is an absolute dollar figure — it does not say how much capital was required to earn it. Rank a queue of projects by NPV alone and you will fund the biggest projects first, which can burn through a limited budget before smaller, more capital-efficient projects even get considered.

Profitability Index (PI) = PV of Future Cash Flows ÷ Initial Outlay

PI measures value created per dollar invested, not value in absolute terms. Under a fixed budget, ranking by PI and funding down the list until the budget runs out maximizes total portfolio NPV — ranking by NPV alone does not, and the gap between the two approaches is not theoretical.

A concrete case

A $310,000 project returning 1.99x per dollar invested and a $1,680,000 project returning 1.75x per dollar invested and four times the absolute NPV: ranked by size, the larger project gets funded first and can consume a third of the budget before anything else is even considered. Ranked by PI, the smaller, more efficient project is funded first — and because the budget is the binding constraint, that ordering funds more total value across the full queue, not less.

One WACC Does Not Fit Every Project

A single company-wide discount rate assumes every project carries the same risk — a maintenance capex request and a new-market expansion are not the same bet, and discounting them at the same rate systematically favors the riskier one (its cash flows get the same "discount" for risk that a much safer project gets). A properly built hurdle-rate structure starts from a cost of capital built up from its components — CAPM cost of equity, relevered beta for the project's actual risk profile, after-tax cost of debt, target capital weights — then adds an explicit risk premium on top for each project's risk class, so a risky project has to clear a higher bar than a safe one before it competes for the same dollar.

Depreciation Is Not a Rounding Detail

After-tax free cash flow depends on the depreciation method, because depreciation shields taxable income even though it is not a cash outflow itself. Straight-line, declining-balance, and MACRS (the US tax system's own accelerated schedule, with distinct class lives and a half-year convention) produce materially different tax-shield timing for the identical asset — and MACRS specifically requires Section 179 and bonus depreciation elections, loss carryforward treatment, and correct salvage-value taxation on the gain over remaining book value to model correctly. Getting this wrong does not change whether a project is worth doing; it changes exactly when the cash shows up, which is exactly what a budget-constrained ranking is sensitive to.

Real Options: What Flexibility Is Worth

A static NPV assumes management makes no decisions after the initial investment. In reality, most projects carry embedded flexibility that has real value: the option to defer investment until more information arrives, to abandon a failing project and recover salvage value, or to expand a successful pilot. Valuing that flexibility as a real option — conceptually similar to a financial option, priced off the same volatility-and-time logic — can turn a marginal-looking static NPV into a clearly positive decision, particularly for staged or pilot-then-scale investments.

A Worked Result

In the Capital Allocation System's shipped worked cycle — 20 projects requesting $14,820,000 against a $4,500,000 budget — the second most valuable single submission in the entire queue, worth $1,055,183 of NPV and comfortably clearing its hurdle rate, gets nothing, because its $2,100,000 capex will not fit the $885,000 left when the PI-based ranking reaches it. A smaller project needing only $540,000 gets funded instead. That is the correct decision under the constraint — and it is also the strongest, most concrete argument a finance team will ever have for asking the budget itself to be increased.

At a Glance

Capital budgeting evaluates a single project with NPV (discounted future cash flows minus initial outlay) and IRR (the discount rate that zeroes NPV). Capital allocation extends this to a queue of competing projects under a fixed budget: ranking by profitability index (value created per dollar invested), not by absolute NPV, maximizes total portfolio value when capital is the binding constraint. A defensible build also assigns each project a risk-adjusted hurdle rate rather than one company-wide WACC, models depreciation correctly (straight-line, declining-balance or MACRS with Section 179 and bonus depreciation), and can value embedded flexibility as real options.
