What a Cap Table Actually Tracks

SAFE conversion, the option pool shuffle, and how a liquidation preference waterfall decides who actually gets paid at exit — explained by a Financial Controller.

By Hoda Elmorshidy • Fractional CFO • Financial Modeling • Corporate Finance since 2017

A cap table is a ledger of who owns what, but the number that matters is not today's ownership percentage — it is what that percentage becomes after every future round, option grant, and conversion event dilutes it. A spreadsheet that only shows current ownership is a snapshot. A cap table model shows the trajectory.

How a SAFE Actually Converts

A SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is not equity yet — it is a promise to convert into equity at a future priced round, on whichever term is more favorable to the investor: the valuation cap or the discount rate.

Conversion Price = MIN( Cap Price, Discounted Round Price )
Cap Price = SAFE Valuation Cap ÷ Pre-Money Fully-Diluted Shares
Discounted Round Price = Priced-Round Price × (1 − Discount %)

The post-money SAFE (the YC 2018 form most seed deals now use) also fixes the investor's ownership percentage independent of how much other SAFE money comes in alongside it — which sounds like a detail and is actually the reason two founders who raised the "same" amount on SAFEs can end up with very different dilution once every SAFE converts at the same priced round.

The Option Pool Shuffle

Before a priced round closes, investors typically require the company to expand its option pool — and that expansion is added to the pre-money valuation, not the post-money.

Why this dilutes founders, not investors

Because the new pool shares come out of the pre-money share count, the new investor's post-money percentage is protected — the dilution from the pool increase lands entirely on the existing founders and earlier investors. A 10% pool top-up structured this way is a meaningfully larger effective dilution to a founder than a simple back-of-envelope 10% suggests, because it compounds with the round's own dilution rather than sitting alongside it. Solving this correctly in closed form (rather than by iterative goal-seek) is one of the more error-prone parts of a DIY cap table.

Two Very Different Exit Waterfalls

At exit, proceeds do not simply split pro-rata by ownership percentage — not if any round carries a liquidation preference.

  • As-converted pro-rata — every share class converts to common and splits proceeds by ownership %. This is the outcome only in a large exit, once every preferred holder is better off converting than taking their preference.
  • Liquidation preference waterfall — each preferred class is paid its preference (commonly 1x the amount invested) before common gets anything, in reverse seniority order (last money in is typically first money out). A participating preferred class then also shares in what's left pro-rata; a non-participating class has to choose between taking its preference or converting to common and giving up the preference — whichever is worth more to them.

The waterfall that actually governs an exit is whichever of the two produces more for each class at the specific exit valuation in question — which is exactly why a real model runs both and compares them at every hypothetical exit value, rather than assuming the friendlier one applies.

MoIC and IRR Belong on a Cap Table Too

Ownership percentage answers "how much of the company do I own." It does not answer "was this a good investment." Per-investor MoIC (proceeds ÷ capital invested) and annualized IRR, calculated from each investor's actual entry round and actual exit proceeds under the waterfall that applies, are what turn a cap table from an ownership ledger into a return analysis.
