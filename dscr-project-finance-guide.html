How a Lender Actually Sizes Project Finance Debt

DSCR sculpting, LLCR and PLCR, the DSRA, and covenant testing — and why a P50 production case is the wrong number to size debt against. Explained by a Financial Controller.

By Hoda Elmorshidy • Fractional CFO • Financial Modeling • Corporate Finance since 2017

A project finance loan is repaid entirely from the cash flow of the asset it finances — there is no parent balance sheet standing behind it. That single fact is why project finance debt sizing looks nothing like a corporate loan: the lender is not asking "can this company service its total debt," they are asking "does this specific asset generate enough cash, in its worst plausible year, to cover this specific loan."

DSCR: The Ratio Everything Else Is Built Around

DSCR = Cash Available for Debt Service ÷ Total Debt Service (Principal + Interest)

A DSCR of 1.40x means the project generates $1.40 of available cash for every $1.00 of scheduled debt service — a 40-cent cushion. Lenders set a minimum DSCR covenant (commonly 1.20x–1.35x for contracted renewable assets) below which the loan is technically in default, and they size the loan amount itself so the DSCR never falls below a target minimum across the entire debt tenor, not just in an average year.

Sculpting the Debt, Not Just Amortizing It

A naive model amortizes debt on a level schedule and checks whether DSCR happens to clear the covenant every year. A lender-grade model works backward: given the project's cash flow profile in every year of the tenor, size each year's principal repayment so DSCR lands as close as possible to the target minimum in every period — higher repayment in strong cash-flow years, lower in weak ones. This is "sculpting," and it is why the debt balance in a real project finance model rarely amortizes on a straight line.

Why the sculpted minimum and the covenant minimum can differ

A model can sculpt to a 1.40x target and still show a 1.56x minimum across the full tenor, because sculpting works off the base-case cash flow profile while the covenant test runs against a sensitized downside. If the downside case shows a period where DSCR would fall below the 1.40x sculpting target, the debt gets sized smaller until the covenant minimum clears — which is exactly why the number a lender actually cares about is the minimum across the full tenor under stress, not the average, and not the target used to build the schedule.

LLCR and PLCR: Looking Beyond the Next Payment

DSCR is a single-period ratio — it says nothing about whether next year is worse. Two coverage ratios look further ahead:

LLCR = PV(remaining cash flow to loan maturity, discounted at the loan rate) ÷ Outstanding Debt Balance
PLCR = PV(remaining cash flow to project life end) ÷ Outstanding Debt Balance

LLCR (Loan Life Coverage Ratio) asks: over everything left until the loan is repaid, is there enough discounted cash flow to cover the balance? PLCR (Project Life Coverage Ratio) asks the same question over the asset's full remaining useful life, including any tail after the loan is repaid. A project can show a healthy annual DSCR every year and still have a thin LLCR if cash flow is backloaded near maturity — which is precisely the scenario LLCR exists to catch.

The DSRA: A Cushion Funded Up Front

A Debt Service Reserve Account (DSRA) is a cash reserve, funded at or before financial close, sized to cover a set number of months of forward debt service (commonly six). It exists so a single bad quarter — a maintenance outage, a temporary curtailment — does not immediately trip a payment default; the DSRA covers the gap while the underlying issue gets fixed. Sizing it too small defeats its purpose; sizing it too large ties up equity capital that could otherwise reduce the size of the loan needed.

What Actually Happens When DSCR Slips

Covenant testing is not binary pass/fail — a real facility agreement defines an escalating sequence:

  1. Lock-up — DSCR falls below a distribution threshold (but above default). Cash distributions to equity are trapped in the project until DSCR recovers.
  2. Cure — DSCR falls further, into a defined cure window. The sponsor has a contractual right to inject equity or subordinated cash to bring the ratio back above the threshold before it becomes a default.
  3. Event of Default — DSCR remains below the floor after the cure period expires, or a cure right is exhausted. Lenders gain enforcement rights.

A model that only shows a single covenant pass/fail line is not modeling the actual facility agreement — it is modeling a simplification of it.

Why Debt Is Sized on a Downside Case, Not P50

P50 production (the level exceeded in 50% of years) is the right number for an equity investor's base-case return expectation. It is the wrong number to size debt against, because by definition the asset underperforms P50 roughly half the time. Lenders size debt against a more conservative exceedance probability — commonly P90 or a multi-year P99 — specifically because debt service is a fixed, non-negotiable obligation and the lender needs coverage to hold even in a year the asset genuinely underperforms.

At a Glance

Project finance debt is sized by "sculpting" principal repayment so the Debt Service Coverage Ratio (cash available for debt service ÷ total debt service) holds at or above a target minimum across the full tenor under a conservative downside production case (commonly P90–P99), not the P50 base case used for equity returns. LLCR and PLCR extend the coverage test across the remaining loan life and full project life; a funded DSRA covers a set number of months of debt service if cash flow dips; and a lock-up/cure/event-of-default covenant ladder governs what happens if DSCR slips.
