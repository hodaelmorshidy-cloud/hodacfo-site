What Changes Under IFRS 18 — and How to Prepare the Transition

The five-category income statement, two new required subtotals, and formal disclosure of the non-GAAP measures you already publish — effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027, explained by a Financial Controller.

By Hoda Elmorshidy • Fractional CFO • Financial Modeling • Corporate Finance since 2017

In April 2024 the IASB issued IFRS 18 Presentation and Disclosure in Financial Statements, replacing IAS 1 — the standard that has governed how an income statement is laid out for decades. It is effective for annual reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027, with earlier application permitted, and it applies retrospectively under IAS 8: the comparative period is restated, and a reconciliation between the restated and previously presented comparative amounts is required. For a calendar-year reporter, that means the FY2026 figures you are closing now will be presented again in 2027 — reclassified under the new rules. The transition is not an accounting-policy change in the measurement sense; almost no number changes. It is a presentation change that touches every line of the P&L, and the judgement calls sit in the classification, not the arithmetic.

The Five-Category Income Statement

IFRS 18 classifies every item of income and expense in the statement of profit or loss into one of five categories:

  • Operating — income and expenses from the entity's main business activities, plus anything not classified elsewhere. Operating is the residual category: it is defined by what it is not, which is why unexpected items land there by default.
  • Investing — income and expenses from assets that generate a return largely independently of the entity's other resources: equity-accounted associates and joint ventures, most investment property, and similar stand-alone return generators.
  • Financing — income and expenses from liabilities that arise from raising finance, and interest on other liabilities.
  • Income taxes — as accounted for under IAS 12.
  • Discontinued operations — as presented under IFRS 5.

The first three categories are the new machinery. Under IAS 1, an entity chose where items like interest income, associates' results, or foreign-exchange differences sat, and two peers in the same industry could present the same economics in structurally different statements. IFRS 18's purpose is to end that: the categories are defined, and comparability is the point.

Two New Required Subtotals

IFRS 18 requires two defined subtotals in the statement of profit or loss: operating profit, and profit before financing and income taxes. Neither was required under IAS 1 — "operating profit" appeared in most published statements, but each entity drew its own boundary around it. Now the boundary is the operating category itself, so any line misclassified between categories moves a required subtotal.

Why classification errors are visible errors

Under IAS 1, putting a gain in the wrong section of the P&L rarely changed a defined number. Under IFRS 18, the same mistake moves operating profit or profit before financing and income taxes — both defined, both comparable across entities, and both the first place an auditor or analyst will look. A classification exercise that used to be a formatting discussion is now a compliance one.

Two reclassifications show up wrong repeatedly in draft restatements. The share of profit of equity-accounted associates and joint ventures moves to the investing category — the "integral to the main business" test from the 2019 exposure draft did not survive into the final standard, so this applies to every entity. And a gain or loss on disposal of property, plant and equipment stays in operating — it reads like a one-off capital item, but PPE is an operating asset and its disposal result follows it. Banks, Insurers, and Other "Specified Main Business Activity" Entities

The five-category model bends for entities whose main business is investing in assets or providing financing to customers. A retail bank's interest income is not an incidental financing item — it is the operating business — so IFRS 18 requires such entities to classify income and expenses differently from a manufacturer, moving items that would otherwise sit in investing or financing into operating. Whether an entity has a specified main business activity, and which items move, is one of the genuinely judgemental parts of the transition, and it is entity-wide: the answer reshapes the whole statement, not one line. MPMs: Your Non-GAAP Measures Move Into the Audited Notes

The disclosure change with the widest organisational reach is the management-defined performance measure regime. An MPM is a subtotal of income and expenses, not specified by IFRS Accounting Standards, that the entity uses in public communications to communicate management's view of financial performance — adjusted EBITDA, adjusted operating profit, underlying earnings, and their relatives. Under IFRS 18 these measures must be disclosed in the financial statements, reconciled to the most directly comparable IFRS-specified subtotal.

The practical consequence: a measure that today lives in the investor presentation, outside the audit's reach, moves into a note inside the audited financial statements. That demands an inventory of every performance measure the company uses externally, a decision on which meet the MPM definition, and a reconciliation for each that will survive audit — including the tax and non-controlling-interest effects of the adjusting items. The kit's MPM Register ships three fully worked reconciliations (Adjusted EBITDA, Adjusted Operating Profit, Adjusted Profit Before Tax) with the IFRS 18.123(d) tax-and-NCI effect lines laid out, each stating the method used so the same lines can be computed for your own measures.

Aggregation and Disaggregation

IFRS 18 also adds principles for how items are grouped: items are aggregated on the basis of shared characteristics and disaggregated where the differences are material, with pressure on opaque residual captions. In practice this is the workstream that reaches deepest into the chart of accounts — a large "other operating expenses" line that survived under IAS 1 will attract questions under IFRS 18, and answering them may require account-level detail the ledger does not currently tag.

How to Prepare: The Transition as a Project

Measured against a first annual period beginning 1 January 2027 with a restated FY2026 comparative, the working window is short — the comparative year is already underway. A transition plan that survives contact with an audit committee has a recognisable shape:

  1. Classify the entity first. The specified-main-business-activity question changes every downstream mapping, so it is decided once, documented, and signed off before any line-level work.
  2. Map the income statement line by line. Every existing P&L or trial-balance line gets an IFRS 18 category and a paragraph basis. The test of a finished mapping is a tie-out: the five categories must re-sum exactly to the profit you already report.
  3. Run a gap analysis against the disclosure requirements. Identify what the notes must newly contain — MPM reconciliations, disaggregation detail, the comparative reconciliation — and score each gap by the effort to close it.
  4. Inventory and reconcile the MPMs. Involve investor relations early; the definition turns on what is used in public communications, which finance does not fully control.
  5. Restate the comparative and brief the board. The restated comparative needs the same rigour as a reported period, and the audit committee should see the reclassified shape of the P&L — and how operating profit moves — well before the market does.

What a Template Cannot Decide for You

No workbook makes the judgement calls: whether your entity has a specified main business activity, whether a given measure meets the MPM definition, how far to disaggregate. Those are decisions to make with your auditor, documented against the standard's own paragraphs — and if your jurisdiction endorses IFRS Accounting Standards locally, against the endorsed text and its status. What a well-built kit does is remove the mechanical failure modes: the line left unmapped, the subtotal that does not tie, the reconciliation missing its tax effect, the reclassification done backwards because the 2019 exposure draft said something the final standard does not. That is mechanical rigour, and it is exactly what a spreadsheet is for.
