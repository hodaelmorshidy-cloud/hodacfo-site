SaaS Financial Projections in Excel: From MRR Waterfall to Cash

The projection architecture recurring-revenue businesses actually need — the MRR waterfall, NRR/GRR, CAC payback — and why generic templates get SaaS wrong.

A SaaS financial projection is not a revenue line with a growth rate. Recurring revenue is a stock, not a flow: this month’s revenue is last month’s, plus what you added, minus what you lost. Any model that projects “revenue × (1+g)” has already failed — it cannot tell expansion from acquisition, and those two have completely different costs.

The MRR waterfall is the engine

  • Opening MRR → + new business → + expansion → − contraction → − churn → closing MRR. Every month, every segment.
  • GRR (gross revenue retention) excludes expansion: it answers “how leaky is the bucket?” Capped at 100% by construction.
  • NRR (net revenue retention) includes expansion: above 100% means the installed base grows without any new logo — the single number sophisticated readers check first.
  • Dollar-based, not logo-based, for both — losing ten tiny accounts and one enterprise account are different events.

From MRR to the three statements

Bookings are not revenue; revenue is not cash. Annual prepay creates deferred revenue — cash today, revenue over twelve months. A SaaS projection that skips the deferred-revenue bridge will show a company dying of losses while its bank balance grows, or the reverse. The bridge is where the P&L meets reality.

Unit economics that must be printed

  • CAC payback in months, on gross-margin dollars — not on revenue.
  • LTV/CAC with LTV derived from the churn the waterfall actually shows — not an aspirational 3% when the model churns 5%.
  • Burn multiple: net burn ÷ net new ARR — the capital-efficiency number an investor computes anyway; compute it first.

The traps

1. Churn as an annual percentage in a monthly model

10% annual churn is not 0.83% monthly compounding — period mixing here silently rewrites retention. Convert once, in a labeled cell, and drive everything monthly.

2. Expansion booked as new business

It flatters CAC (expansion has near-zero acquisition cost) and hides bucket leaks. The waterfall exists to keep the two apart.

3. Headcount that does not reconcile

A hiring plan whose salary total does not tie to the P&L’s payroll line is two models disagreeing in one file. The Annual Budget & Reforecast Model ships a check column whose only job is proving that tie — the same discipline belongs in every SaaS plan.
