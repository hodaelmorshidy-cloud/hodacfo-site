What Actually Happens Inside an LBO Model

Sources & Uses, the multi-tranche debt stack, the cash sweep, and how paying down debt turns into an equity return — explained by a Financial Controller.

By Hoda Elmorshidy • Fractional CFO • Financial Modeling • Corporate Finance since 2017

A leveraged buyout (LBO) model answers one question: if a sponsor buys a company mostly with borrowed money, pays that debt down with the company's own cash flow over a multi-year hold, and sells at exit, what return did the equity check actually earn? The mechanics are simple to describe and easy to get wrong in a spreadsheet, because the debt stack, the cash sweep, and the returns calculation are all interdependent.

Sources & Uses: The Balance Check Everything Else Depends On

Every LBO starts with the entry price, usually expressed as a multiple of EBITDA, and a Sources & Uses schedule that must balance exactly to zero.

Uses = Purchase Enterprise Value + Transaction Fees + Refinanced Debt
Sources = New Debt Tranches + Sponsor Equity Check
Sources − Uses = 0

The equity check is the plug: once the debt tranches are sized against leverage and coverage limits, whatever the debt does not cover comes from the sponsor's equity. Get the leverage assumption wrong and every downstream number — the debt balance, the interest expense, the equity value at exit — is wrong along with it.

Why a Real LBO Uses More Than One Tranche

A single blended debt number is a simplification that free templates lean on because it is easy to build. A real capital structure stacks tranches by seniority, each with its own rate, amortization schedule, and claim on cash:

  • Senior term loan — lowest cost, first claim on repayment, mandatory annual amortization
  • Mezzanine debt — subordinated, higher rate, sits behind the senior tranche
  • Accreting PIK note — pays no cash interest; the coupon accretes onto principal instead, which changes the balance the sponsor has to retire before equity value is created
  • Revolver — drawn against a minimum-cash floor when operating cash is tight, repaid first when it is not

Stacking tranches this way is what makes the debt paydown story realistic: the senior loan amortizes on schedule regardless of performance, the mezzanine and PIK layers carry the risk premium a real lender would price in, and the minimum-cash floor stops the model from sweeping the company into a liquidity problem.

The Cash Sweep Waterfall

Free cash flow generated each year has to go somewhere, in a fixed order of priority:

1. Mandatory senior amortization (contractual, non-negotiable)
2. Cash interest on each tranche (senior, then mezzanine)
3. PIK coupon accretes to principal (non-cash, no FCF used)
4. Remaining free cash flow → 100% cash sweep, applied to the senior tranche

The cash sweep is the mechanism that compounds returns for a leveraged deal: debt paydown that would otherwise take a decade on a fixed amortization schedule accelerates whenever the business generates cash above its mandatory obligations. This is also the single most common source of circular-reference errors in a DIY build, because the sweep depends on free cash flow, and free cash flow includes interest expense, which depends on the ending debt balance the sweep is trying to calculate.

The fix that removes the circularity

Charge interest on the beginning balance of each tranche, not the ending balance. The beginning balance is already known at the start of the period, so interest expense, free cash flow, and the sweep can all be calculated in a single pass with no iterative calculation setting and no circular-reference warning.

Optional Dividend Recaps and Hold Period

A sponsor does not have to wait for exit to realize value. If leverage capacity opens up mid-hold — the debt balance has paid down enough to support a new draw — a dividend recapitalization lets the sponsor take a distribution funded by new debt, resetting leverage higher without selling the company. Modeling this as an optional toggle against a variable one-to-five-year hold period is what lets the same workbook answer "what if we exit in year 3" and "what if we recap in year 2 and exit in year 5" without rebuilding anything.

How MoIC and IRR Are Actually Calculated

MoIC = Total Cash Returned to Equity ÷ Total Equity Invested
IRR = the discount rate r such that:
    ∑ CFt / (1 + r)t = 0

MoIC tells you the multiple on capital — a 2.5x MoIC means every dollar invested came back as $2.50. It says nothing about time. IRR annualizes the same cash flows, so a 2.5x MoIC over three years and a 2.5x MoIC over seven years produce very different IRRs. A model that reports MoIC without IRR, or IRR without a dated XIRR that accounts for exact holding-period length rather than whole years, is only telling half the story a return needs.

When a Simple Model Is Actually the Right Call

Not every deal needs a four-tranche waterfall. A single-lender, single-tranche acquisition loan with straight-line amortization is genuinely well served by a simple model — the multi-tranche machinery only earns its keep once there is more than one class of debt competing for the same cash flow, which is most institutional buyouts but not every small acquisition.
