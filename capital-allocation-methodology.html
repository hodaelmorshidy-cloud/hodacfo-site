Capital Allocation System — Methodology

Which projects to fund when you cannot fund them all: driver-based cash flows, risk-adjusted hurdles, profitability-index ranking under a fixed budget, and the worked cases that show why — published from the shipped manual.

By Hoda Elmorshidy • Fractional CFO • Financial Modeling • Corporate Finance since 2017 • Published 2026-08-17

The product page carries a machine-verified audit certificate with the shipping file’s SHA-256.

This page publishes the methodology behind the Capital Allocation System. The unit of analysis is the capital budget, not the individual project: a project appraisal answers “is this worth doing?”; a capital allocation answers “given a fixed amount of money and more good ideas than money, where does it go?” The system appraises up to twenty projects over a twenty-five-year horizon, discounts each at its own risk-adjusted hurdle, ranks by value created per unit of capital, and funds down the ranking until the budget is exhausted — then reports what the unfunded projects would have been worth, usually the most useful number in the pack.

Building the Cash Flows

FCF(0) = −(capex + working capital on year-1 revenue)
FCF(t) = EBIT(t) × (1 − tax) + depreciation(t) − ΔWC(t)
FCF(n) = the above + after-tax salvage + release of working capital

Every project starts as drivers, not a typed cash-flow row. Depreciation is deducted to compute taxable profit and added straight back — its only cash effect is the tax it shelters, which is exactly why the method matters to NPV.

Depreciation — Why the Method Changes the Answer

Straight line, double declining balance, and US MACRS are supported. The MACRS GDS half-year tables are derived from the documented method (200% DB for 3–10-year property, 150% for 15–20-year, switching to straight line in the year that gives the larger deduction) and reconcile to IRS Publication 946, Appendix A, Table A-1 within the published two-decimal rounding, checked August 2026. Section 179 and bonus depreciation apply first, in year one. Salvage is taxed on the gain over written-down book value — an asset depreciated straight-line to salvage shows no gain; the same asset under MACRS is written to zero, so the entire proceeds are taxable. A real cash difference, handled explicitly.

Cost of Capital and Risk-Adjusted Hurdles

Levered beta = unlevered beta × (1 + (1 − tax) × D/E)
Cost of equity = risk-free + levered beta × ERP + premiums
WACC = We × Re + Wd × Rd × (1 − tax)

One hurdle for every project is a common and expensive simplification — discounting a replacement compressor and a speculative new market at the same WACC systematically over-funds the speculative one. Five risk classes carry explicit premiums over WACC (maintenance 0.0% → new market 5.0%); any project can override its class rate. The premiums are a judgment call, yours to set and defend — what matters is that they are explicit, consistent, and visible.

Metrics, and What Each One Hides

NPV = Σ FCF(t) / (1 + r)^t  ·  PI = PV of inflows / initial investment
EAC = NPV × r / (1 − (1 + r)^−n)  ·  real rate = (1 + nominal)/(1 + inflation) − 1

NPV is the only metric that maps directly onto value created. IRR flatters small projects and can have multiple roots; MIRR is almost always lower and more honest. PI — NPV per unit of capital — is the correct ranking when the budget is the constraint. EAC is the only fair comparison across different lives. Payback should never decide anything alone. When the rankings disagree, the disagreement is the finding — put it in the memo. Mid-year discounting and the real-vs-nominal Fisher switch are both explicit; mixing real cash flows with a nominal rate understates every project and is among the most common capital-budgeting errors.

Capital Rationing — the Worked Cases

The system ranks eligible projects by PI and funds down the list. The shipped sample makes the argument concrete:

The greedy PI ranking is not provably optimal; the included Python toolkit has an exact solver that reports when the two differ. Real options are modelled as a transparent two-state decision tree — defer, stage, expand, abandon — with the stated caution to count only options you could genuinely exercise. The Post-Investment Review enters actuals against plan to test whether the appraisal process is calibrated: if growth projects come in short three cycles running, the fix is a higher class-5 premium, not better project selection.
